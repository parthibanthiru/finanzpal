The right custody account for your ETF savings plan

Not every account that wins a broker test is also the best one for monthly ETF savings plans. Here's what actually matters.

DL
David Lang
Updated October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

A broker comparison usually looks at the order fee per individual trade — relevant if you occasionally invest larger sums. For a monthly ETF savings plan, though, different criteria matter: how many ETFs can you save into for free, how low can your monthly contribution be, and what happens to the cash sitting in your settlement account between execution dates?

1. Free ETF savings plans: not every ETF counts

Providers like to advertise "thousands of ETF savings plans available" — the number of commission-free ones at the larger custody accounts is usually in the one- to three-thousand range. What matters isn't the total count, though, but whether the specific ETF you want to invest in (say, a particular MSCI World or All-World ETF) is actually included for free. At some providers, only part of the range is commission-free; the rest costs a regular order fee per execution.

2. Minimum contribution: possible from €1, rarely ideal

Several custody accounts now allow ETF savings plans from as little as €1 a month — handy for trying out how a savings plan feels, with no commitment. For long-term wealth building, though, a higher, regular contribution that fits your budget usually makes more sense. A low minimum matters most if you want to invest in several ETFs at once or pause flexibly.

GOOD TO KNOW

Some custody accounts pay interest on cash sitting uninvested in your settlement account, others don't, or only temporarily as part of a new-customer promotion. If you tend to hold larger cash reserves in your account, this figure is worth checking.

3. Keep custody fees and minimum deposits in view

An ongoing custody fee on your balance, or a minimum deposit below which fees apply, can noticeably dent returns over the years — especially for smaller accounts. These items rarely feature prominently in advertising, but they're part of the full cost picture.
